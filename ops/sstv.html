Slow-scan TV pictures

Twice an hour, at :20 and :50, the beacon sends one picture by slow-scan television (SSTV). At :20 it's a science picture: the GOES-West satellite view, the sun from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory, HF conditions for the past week and the past month (maximum usable frequency, critical frequency with the NVIS range, solar flux and K index on one chart), K6EEG's tropo forecast map, or a test card. At :50 it's a QSL card: a photo from San Francisco and around. Each is introduced by voice, followed by a countdown in Morse code, 5 to 1, a second apart, so you can start your decoder in time. Every picture carries K6EEG and the time. Below are the originals as sent, with the audio exactly as it went out, so you can compare them with what you decoded (noise shows up as streaks, multipath as ghosting or smearing), or practice decoding from a clean copy.

Pictures follow a fixed six-hour cycle: see the daily schedule. A picture starts on the minute shown, or as soon as the air is clear. If one has nothing to send, a test card goes out instead.

How to receive

Start a decoder app, then hold the phone near the receiver's speaker, or feed the receiver's audio to a computer. The decoder picks the mode by itself from the start tones (the VIS header). Modes in use: Robot 36 (320 by 240, about 38 seconds), PD 120 (640 by 496, about 128 seconds).

Practice

Play any picture's audio on this page (or the test cards below) next to your phone, or download the MP3 and open it in MMSSTV or QSSTV. The test cards never change, so they're a steady reference: the color bars and gray steps show color and level, and the four sets of lines (1 to 4 pixels wide) show how much detail survives.

Apps and resources

Decoders

  • Robot36 for Android: Free and open source; also on F-Droid. Decodes from the phone's microphone.
  • SSTV Slow Scan TV for iPhone and iPad: Black Cat Systems. Decodes from the microphone.
  • MMSSTV for Windows: The classic. Shows the FSK ID (K6EEG) sent after each picture, and can save every picture it receives.
  • QSSTV for Linux: Runs on a Raspberry Pi too; also on Flathub.
  • Web SSTV, in a web browser: No install: works on any computer with a microphone, Macs included (about it, from W2SZ). Check that it handles the mode you want to try.

Learn more

  • SSTV basics, from the Online Amateur Radio Community: How it works, the common modes, and how to get started.
  • CQ SSTV: SSTV news, software and resources.
  • ARISS: SSTV from the International Space Station: The space station sends SSTV pictures on 145.800 MHz during special events; the same apps decode them.
  • SSTV Encoder for Android: Make your own SSTV audio from a photo (for practice, or for your own station).
